Tom came up to the cottage in the afternoon with a box of her father's things from the boat shed: a tobacco tin, a knife, a pair of mittens their mother had knitted forty years ago and he had never let anyone throw away.

'Thought you should have them,' he said, and stood in the kitchen doorway turning his cap.

She made him sit. She made tea in her father's brown pot, which was the only thing in the house that had not changed, and while it brewed she went through the tin. Tobacco, matches, a pencil stub. Under the lining paper, folded small, three sheets of foolscap.

*To the Clerk of the County Fisheries Committee, Truro.*

It was a letter, or the draft of one, in his square hand and his own brown ink. Mara read it standing up. *Sir, I have the honour to bring to your attention certain irregularities at the cannery of Trevaskis and Co., Porthwenna.* Below that, columns: dates, boats, weights landed at the quay as he had recorded them in the ledger, and weights paid for at the cannery. The second column was always smaller. Sometimes by a tenth. Sometimes by a quarter.

'Tom,' she said. 'Did you know about this?'

He took the sheets and held them at arm's length, his lips moving. When he looked up his face had gone grey. 'He talked about it,' he said. 'Short weights. He said she'd been robbing the boats for years, and with her holding everybody's debts there was nobody would dare say so. He said he'd say so.'

'He never sent it.'

'No.' Tom put the letter down on the table very gently, as if it were the man himself. 'He was going to take it to Truro himself. The week after the third.'

Mara thought of Eliza's hands, both held out. *Half the boats in this cove are bought with my money.*

'She knew,' Mara said. 'She must have known he was going to.'

Tom didn't say yes. He didn't say no either.
