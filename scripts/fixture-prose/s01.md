The bus left Mara at the top of the hill and went on without her, its lamps swinging into the dark like something that had somewhere better to be. Below, Porthwenna lay folded into its cove: the harbour wall, the cannery chimney, the slate roofs stepping down to the water. The sea was flat. It had been flat, the telegram said, on the night her father drowned.

She carried her case down the hill herself. Nobody had come to meet her, and she had told nobody which bus.

Jory was on the harbour wall, where she had known he would be. He was sitting with his legs over the drop and a bottle between his knees, and he did not look round when her shoes rang on the stone.

'You came, then,' he said.

'He was my father too.'

'Was he.' Jory drank. 'You'd not know it from your letters. Two a year. Christmas and his birthday.'

She set the case down. The water below them made small sounds against the wall, nothing more. 'Tell me how it happened.'

'Everybody knows how it happened. He went out in the night and the sea had him.' He said it the way people in the village said everything about the sea, as if it were weather, as if it were nobody's fault.

'It was calm, Jory. The telegram said it was calm.'

For a moment he was very still. Then he swung his legs back over the wall and stood, and she saw how thin he had got, how his coat hung off him. 'Funeral's at ten,' he said. 'Chapel, not church. He'd have wanted chapel.'

'He'd have wanted to be alive.'

Jory picked up the bottle, looked at what was left in it, and set it down again on the wall, carefully, the way their father used to set down the ledger at the end of a night's watch. 'Your room's as you left it,' he said. 'I didn't touch anything.'

He walked off along the quay before she could answer. Mara stayed until the lamps came on in the Ship, one window at a time, and the bottle was the only thing on the wall that caught the light.
