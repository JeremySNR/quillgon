At dawn she walked down to the harbour wall and found Jory already there, sitting where he had sat on the first night, legs over the drop, no bottle.

She sat beside him. The tide was at the turn, the water neither coming in nor going out, lying in the cove like something holding its breath. Slack water, their father had called it. The only time the sea tells the truth, he used to say, because it isn't pulling anywhere.

'I signed it,' Jory said. 'The letter. Tom brought it round last night.'

'I know. He told me.'

'They'll all know now. About the *Kittiwake*. About the book.'

'Yes.'

He nodded slowly. Out past the harbour mouth a boat was going out, its engine a small steady sound across the flat water. Pascoe's, probably. Pascoe was always first.

'I'm going to correct the ledger,' Mara said. 'Not tear the page out. Rule a line through it, the way he did when he got a tide wrong, and write the truth underneath and sign it. Calm. Wind light, northerly. Walter Penrose lost at sea going to the aid of a vessel adrift.' She looked at him. 'You can write it, if you want. It should be in a Penrose hand.'

Jory was quiet for a long time.

'His ink,' he said finally. 'If I do it. There's still some in the jar on the shelf. I'll use his ink.'

'All right.'

He drew his legs up off the drop and wrapped his arms round his knees, and for a moment he looked like the boy who used to sit here with their father on summer evenings, counting the boats home.

'Are you staying?' he asked.

Mara watched the water begin, very slightly, to move. 'For a while,' she said. 'Someone has to keep the book.'
