She went to the cannery with Tom beside her and her father's letter in her coat, and this time she did not wait to be shown up.

Eliza was at her desk. She looked at Tom, and then at Mara, and took off her reading glasses and folded them.

'You knew,' Mara said. 'About Jory and the ledger. You knew from the start.'

'I knew the morning after.' Eliza did not pretend. 'He came to me for money, that same week, to pay the yard for the punt. He was crying. He told me everything. I suppose he had to tell someone.'

'And you kept it.'

'I kept it,' Eliza said. 'And when he started talking about your father's papers, about the letter to Truro, I reminded him that I was keeping it. Yes.' She met Mara's eyes. 'That's what I do. I keep things. Debts, mostly.'

Tom made a sound in his throat. Mara put a hand on his arm.

'He was going to report your weights,' she said, 'and you used his own son's grief to make sure nobody else would. That's the whole of it.'

'That's the whole of it,' Eliza agreed. 'What will you do?'

Mara laid the letter on the desk. 'I'll finish it. I've added the figures for this winter from the quay. Tom's signed as a witness, and so have the Hockings and the Pascoes. Jory's going to sign too, about the ledger and about you. It goes to Truro on Monday.' She paused. 'You could pay the boats back first. Every short weight, out of the ledger. It might go easier.'

Eliza looked at the letter for a long time. Down on the floor the women were singing something, the way they did on long shifts, a hymn with the words worn off it.

'Your father,' Eliza said at last, 'would have given me that choice too.'

'Yes,' said Mara. 'That's why I'm giving it to you.'
