The cannery had grown since she left. There was a new wing in raw brick and a sign over the gate, TREVASKIS & CO., in letters that had been gilded once and were already going green with salt. Inside, the women at the long tables did not look up from the pilchards.

Eliza Trevaskis received her in an office above the floor, with a window that looked down on all of it. She was older than Mara remembered and better dressed than anyone in the county, and she held out both hands as if they were old friends.

'Mara. I'm so sorry. Your father was the most honest man in Porthwenna.'

'People keep telling me that.'

'Because it's true.' Eliza poured tea without asking. 'If there's anything you need. The funeral costs, the house. Walter and I had our disagreements, but I don't let disagreements outlive people.'

'What disagreements?'

A small pause. 'Business. The harbour dues. He thought I had too much say in the village, and I suppose I do.' She smiled. 'Half the boats in this cove are bought with my money, Mara. Their nets, their engines, their winter. I hold the paper on most of them. It isn't a secret.'

'And they sell their catch to you.'

'Where else would they sell it?'

Mara put her cup down. 'There was no storm on the third,' she said. 'Tom Kessell says the bay was flat. My father's ledger says force eight.'

Something moved behind Eliza's eyes and went still again, quick as a fish turning under the surface. 'Grief does strange things to a hand,' she said gently. 'He was not young. Perhaps he was confused.'

'He wasn't confused for thirty-one years.'

'Then perhaps you should leave it be.' Eliza rose; the interview was over. At the door she touched Mara's arm. 'This is a small place,' she said. 'Questions travel. People here owe a great deal, and they don't like to be reminded of it. Go home after the funeral, Mara. It would be kinder.'
