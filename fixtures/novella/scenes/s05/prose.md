She found Jory in the harbour office the next morning, standing over the desk with his hands in his pockets, looking at the ledger as if it were a dog that might bite.

'What are you doing here?'

'It's my office too,' he said. 'Was going to be. He was teaching me the book.'

Mara crossed to the window and pulled the shutter back. Grey light fell across the page. She had not slept; she had lain awake with Eliza's voice in her head and her father's handwriting behind her eyes, and somewhere around four she had understood what had been bothering her.

'Look at this,' she said.

Jory didn't move.

'Look, Jory.' She tilted the ledger to the light. 'Every entry for thirty-one years is in his iron-gall ink. It goes brown as it ages. See, here, February, it's already browning. And here.' She put her finger on the third of March. 'Force eight. Heavy sea. That's blue-black. That's the bottled stuff from Hocking's, the stuff you use for your pools coupons.'

'Lots of people buy ink from Hocking's.'

'Not Dad. Dad mixed his own. He was proud of it.' She looked at him. 'And the hand's wrong. The sevens. He crossed his sevens, he learned that from the Norwegian skippers. These aren't crossed.'

Jory took his hands out of his pockets and put them back in. 'So he was in a hurry.'

'In a hurry, at night, in a storm that didn't happen, he went out and bought new ink.'

'I don't know, Mara.' His voice cracked on her name. 'I don't know what you want me to say. He's dead. He went out and he's dead and you weren't here, and now you're here with your inks and your sevens like it's a puzzle in the Sunday paper—'

'It was calm,' she said. 'Somebody wrote a storm into his book.'

Jory walked out without answering. The door banged on its hinge, once, twice, and stood open to the quay.
