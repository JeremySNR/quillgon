Tom Kessell was mending a net on the quay with his back to the wind, which was nothing much, a breath off the land. He had been her father's deckhand for twenty years and he looked up at her the way he had when she was nine and had fallen off the slip: carefully, as if she might break further.

'Miss Mara.'

'Just Mara, Tom. Please.' She sat on an upturned fish box beside him. 'I read the ledger last night.'

His hands kept working the twine. 'Did you.'

'It says force eight on the third. A heavy sea. Boats warned.'

Tom finished the knot he was on, and then another, and only then put the needle down. 'Your father wrote that?'

'It's in the book.'

'There was no weather that night,' Tom said. 'Not a breath. I was up at two with my chest and I stood at the window and the bay was like a plate. You could have rowed to France.' He looked at the water, then back at her. 'Ask anybody. Ask the lifeboat men. Nobody was warned because there was nothing to warn about.'

'Then why would he write it?'

'He wouldn't.' Tom said it flatly. 'Not Walter. He'd sooner have cut off his hand than put a lie in that book. You know that.'

She did know it. That was what frightened her.

A gull landed on the bollard and watched them. Tom picked up his needle again, but he did not go on mending. He seemed to be weighing something, the way he weighed a catch with his eyes before it went on the scales.

'You should talk to your brother,' he said finally.

'I've talked to Jory. He says the sea had him.'

'Aye.' Tom drew the twine tight. 'Well. Jory says a lot of things, these days.' He would not say any more than that, and when she pressed him he only shook his head and told her the tide was turning and he had pots to see to.
