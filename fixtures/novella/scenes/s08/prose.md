Eliza sent a boy with a note, and Mara went, because she wanted to see Eliza's face when she said what she had come to say.

This time there was no tea. Eliza stood at the window above the cannery floor with a folder under her arm, and she turned as Mara came in with the smile already arranged.

'I've a proposal,' she said. 'The harbour house. It's too big for one, and Jory can't keep it. I'll buy it at a fair price, more than fair, and clear your father's account at the chandler's into the bargain. You'd go back to London with something in your pocket.'

'My father had an account at the chandler's?'

'Everybody does. Everybody owes somebody, Mara. In Porthwenna they mostly owe me.' She said it without any particular pride, as if reporting the tide. 'I'm offering to make one family's debts disappear. Not many people get that.'

Mara took the folded foolscap from her coat and laid it on the desk between them. 'He was writing to the County Fisheries Committee,' she said. 'About your weights. Landed at the quay, paid at the gate. He had it all, column by column, out of the ledger. He was going to take it to Truro the week after he drowned.'

Eliza did not pick it up. She looked at it for a long moment, and when she lifted her eyes again the smile was gone, and what was underneath it was not fear exactly but a kind of tiredness, as if she had been waiting for this conversation for a long time.

'Your father,' she said, 'was a good man, and a stubborn one, and he did not understand how a village like this stays alive in a winter. I lend. They fish. Some years they can't pay and I carry them. The weights pay for the carrying.' She pushed the letter back across the desk with one finger. 'Take your offer or leave it. But I didn't drown him, Mara. Whatever you think. Ask your brother where he was that night.'
