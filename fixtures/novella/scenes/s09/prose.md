Tom was waiting for her on the quay at dawn, as if they had arranged it. Perhaps they had, without saying so. The tide was out and the boats lay over on their sides in the mud with their masts pointing every way, like a field after wind.

'Eliza said to ask Jory where he was that night,' Mara said.

'Aye. She would.'

'I've been back over the ledger. Every entry for the third in his ink and his hand, right up to nine in the evening. Then the storm, in the blue-black. Somebody sat at that desk after he was dead and wrote it in. Somebody who knew how he set out a page.' She heard her own voice, steady, and wondered at it. 'Who would know that, Tom?'

He looked out past the harbour mouth at the Mewstone, black against a sky going white.

'The *Kittiwake* slipped her mooring that night,' he said. 'Jory's boat. I saw her out past the rock from my window, drifting, no one aboard. Your father went after her in the punt. On his own.' He rubbed his face with both hands. 'He'd have got her too. He'd have had a line on her. But the punt was old, and it was cold, and he wasn't young, Mara. There was no storm. There didn't need to be.'

She stood very still. The mud ticked and bubbled as the water crept back across it.

'He told you this? Jory?'

'I told him. Last night, at the Ship. I said if he didn't tell you, I would.' Tom looked at her at last. 'I'm sorry, girl. I've known since the morning they found him. I should have said at the funeral. I should have said before.'

'Why didn't you?'

'Because he's all that's left of Walter,' Tom said. 'And because I'm a coward. Take your pick.'
